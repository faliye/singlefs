

In copy-on-write (COW) filesystems like Btrfs or ZFS, `fsync` ensures data durability. Method (A) performs an immediate full checkpoint: it writes all dirty data leaves, their ancestor metadata nodes, and a new root node to stable storage. This guarantees the entire modified subtree is consistent on disk. After a crash, recovery is trivial—the root pointer is valid, and all data is intact. No reconstruction is needed; the filesystem simply validates the root and proceeds.

Method (B) writes only dirty data leaves and a single journal record (e.g., a log of new leaf pointers), deferring ancestor node updates to the next checkpoint. This avoids writing intermediate metadata during `fsync`, reducing latency for the call. However, after a crash, recovery must reconstruct the missing ancestor nodes. The journal record provides the new leaf locations, but the hierarchical metadata linking them to the root is incomplete. Recovery must traverse the journal, rebuild the intermediate nodes (e.g., by reading the journal and recalconstructing parent pointers), and write them to disk before the filesystem can be mounted. This adds significant overhead to crash recovery.

The **main cost of (B)** is this increased recovery time and complexity. While (B) optimizes `fsync` performance for normal operation, it shifts the computational burden to crash recovery. If multiple `fsync` operations occur before a checkpoint, the journal accumulates changes, making recovery exponentially slower. This trade-off—faster `fsync` at the expense of slower, more complex crash recovery—makesposes a critical risk for systems where fast boot times after failures are essential.
